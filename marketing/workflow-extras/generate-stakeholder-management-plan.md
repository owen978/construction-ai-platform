## When not to use it
Don't use it instead of talking to people. The plan maps who matters and how you'll engage them, but it's only as good as what you know about the area. Speak to the client, the local authority and the neighbours before you trust a grid.

It's also not the place to handle consents. Section 61 consents, and the construction management plans that planning conditions often ask for, each have their own process with the local authority and may need specialist input. The stakeholder plan should point to them, not try to replace them.

## Common mistakes
- Writing it once and filing it. Stakeholders move: neighbours who barely notice the groundworks will notice the piling.
- Putting almost everyone in "keep informed" and sending them all the same newsletter.
- Not linking it to the programme, so concrete pours, abnormal loads or road closures land on neighbours without warning.
- Forgetting internal stakeholders: the end user, the client's facilities team, the funder's monitoring surveyor and your own directors.
- No single point of contact, so complaints bounce between the site gate, head office and the client, with nobody logging them.

## FAQs
### What is a stakeholder management plan in construction?
A document listing everyone with an interest in or influence over the project, how much each one matters, and how and how often you'll communicate with them. It usually includes a stakeholder register, an influence and interest grid, a communication plan and a way of tracking issues and complaints.

### How do you identify stakeholders on a construction project?
Start with the contract and the planning consent: the client, funders, end users, the design team, building control, highways and anyone named in the planning conditions. Then walk the boundary and think about who will notice the work, such as neighbours, schools, shops, bus routes and utilities. Ask the client and the local authority who they expect to hear from.

### What is a Section 61 consent?
A prior consent from the local authority under the Control of Pollution Act 1974 that agrees working hours, methods and noise controls before the work starts. Working within it gives you some protection against enforcement action over noise. Your contract may also set working hours or obligations to neighbours. Check your contract and take advice.

### Does the Considerate Constructors Scheme need a stakeholder plan?
Registering commits you to the scheme's Code of Considerate Practice, which includes respecting the community. It doesn't give you a set template, but a named contact, regular updates and a complaints log are good evidence when a monitor visits. Check the current code.
