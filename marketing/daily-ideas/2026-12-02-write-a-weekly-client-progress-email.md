## When to use it
Every Friday afternoon, before the client's PM chases you for an update. A good weekly email stops small issues turning into surprises at the monthly meeting, and it builds a record of when you told the client what. The hard part is pulling it together from the diary, the look-ahead and your inbox when you'd rather be on site. This does the sorting and the first draft. You keep the judgement on tone and what goes in writing.

## Prompt
You are an experienced UK construction project manager on a [SIZE AND TYPE OF PROJECT, e.g. 40-unit residential scheme, steel frame]. Write this week's progress email to the client's project manager for the week ending [DATE].

Here is what I have:
- Site diary or notes for the week: [PASTE]
- Look-ahead programme for the next one to two weeks: [PASTE OR SUMMARISE]
- Open issues, RFIs and information awaited: [PASTE]
- Health and safety this week: [PASTE: inspections, observations, any incidents, roles only]
- Last week's email, if I sent one: [PASTE]

Write the email with these sections:
1. Progress this week (short bullets by area or trade, against what last week's email said we would do).
2. Planned next week.
3. Decisions or information needed from the client, each with the date we need it by and what it affects.
4. Health and safety summary.
5. Anything carried over from last week that is still open.

Rules:
- Under 300 words. Plain UK English. Factual, calm, no blame and no sales language.
- Only use facts, dates and figures from my notes. Do not invent progress, percentages or dates.
- If something we said last week would happen has not happened, say so plainly in one line.
- No commercial positions, costs or claims in this email unless I have marked them as OK to share.
- After the email, give me a short list headed "Check before sending" covering anything missing, unclear or that reads as a commitment.

## What it produced
A short email with a one-line opener and five headed sections, written in bullets the client's PM can scan on a phone. Decisions needed sit in their own list with a "needed by" date against each. Underneath, a separate "Check before sending" list of gaps, such as a planned activity with no date or an issue that reads like a promise.

## What you still check
- Every date and activity matches the current programme, not an old look-ahead.
- Nothing in it reads as an instruction, an acceptance or a commitment you don't want on record.
- Delays are described the way your commercial lead is happy with, especially if a notice or early warning is involved.
- People are named by role, and nothing about an individual's performance has crept in.

## Steps
1. On Friday, gather the week's diary, the look-ahead, the open issues list and last week's email.
2. Run the prompt using your company's approved AI tool, then clear the "Check before sending" list.
3. Read it once as the client would, send it, and save a copy to the project file.
