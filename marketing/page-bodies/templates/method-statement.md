## What is a method statement?

A method statement describes how a particular task will be carried out safely on site: the sequence of work, the hazards at each step, the controls, and the people, plant and materials involved. It sits alongside the risk assessment. The risk assessment says what could go wrong; the method statement says how the work will actually be done so that it doesn't.

CDM 2015 and the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 require you to assess risks and plan a safe system of work. A method statement is the usual way to set that out for higher-risk tasks, and principal contractors often require one before work starts.

You may also hear it called a safe system of work. Safe Work Method Statement (SWMS) is the Australian term. In the UK, a method statement and its risk assessment together are usually called a RAMS, and [what is a RAMS](/guides/what-is-a-rams-construction-guide) explains how the two fit.

## What sections should a method statement include?

1. **Project information:** project name, site address, client, principal contractor and a document reference.
2. **Description of works:** what the task is, where it happens on site, its scope and any limits.
3. **People and responsibilities:** the supervisor, the operatives and any specialists, with the training, cards or tickets the task needs.
4. **Plant and equipment:** every machine and tool, with the inspections and certificates it needs.
5. **Materials:** everything to be used, including hazardous substances, linked to the COSHH assessments where they apply.
6. **Risk assessment reference:** the risk assessment or assessments that cover this task's hazards.
7. **Sequence of operations:** each step from set-up to clear-up, with what is done, who does it and the controls in place.
8. **Safety controls:** PPE, exclusion zones, permits to work and environmental controls.
9. **Emergency arrangements and welfare:** first aiders, the assembly point, emergency contacts and the nearest A&E.
10. **Review and sign-off:** who prepared, reviewed and approved it, with dates, and a record of everyone briefed on it.

## What does a filled-in method statement look like?

**Example: illustrative only, not a real job.** It shows the level of detail to aim for. Check every line against your own site, drawings and the principal contractor's rules before using anything like it.

**Task:** core drilling four 150 mm holes through an existing reinforced concrete first-floor slab for new drainage pipes, in an occupied office building being refurbished.

**Description of works:** set out the hole positions from the services drawing, confirm the slab is clear to drill, drill the cores using a rig-mounted diamond drill with water suppression, remove the cores and slurry, and leave each hole protected until the pipes go in and the fire stopping is fitted.

**People:** one supervisor and two operatives trained in diamond drilling, with a briefed banksman for the exclusion zone on the floor below.

**Plant and equipment:** 110V rig-mounted core drill with water feed, wet vacuum, cable avoidance tool and signal generator, barriers and signs for the floor below.

**Materials:** water for suppression only. Concrete dust and slurry contain silica, covered by the COSHH assessment.

**Sequence of operations:**

1. Confirm with the principal contractor that the slab is not post-tensioned and that the structural engineer has accepted the hole positions. Do not drill until both are confirmed in writing.
2. Check the services drawings and scan each position from above and below for embedded cables and pipes. Move any hole that clashes, with the engineer's agreement.
3. Set up the exclusion zone on the floor below each hole, with barriers, signs and the banksman in place before drilling starts.
4. Fix the drill rig, connect the water feed and wet vacuum, and drill each core at the manufacturer's recommended speed. Stop and reassess if steel is struck unexpectedly.
5. Catch and remove each core so it cannot fall, vacuum the slurry as you go and keep cables clear of standing water.
6. Cover each hole straight away and leave it marked until the pipe and fire stopping are fitted.

**PPE:** hard hat, safety boots, hi-vis, safety glasses, gloves, hearing protection, and FFP3 masks if any dry cutting can't be avoided.

**Emergency and welfare:** first aider, assembly point and nearest A&E as set out in the construction phase plan, recorded on the sheet before work starts.

**Sign-off:** prepared by the supervisor, reviewed by the contractor's manager, accepted by the principal contractor, and briefed to all three operatives, who sign the briefing record.

## When do you need a method statement?

- Before higher-risk work, such as working at height, hot works, confined spaces, demolition, excavation, lifting operations and work near live services.
- When the principal contractor or client asks for one as a site rule, which most do.
- When a subcontractor mobilises to site.
- When the scope, sequence, plant or site conditions change from what was planned.
- After an incident or near miss shows the existing method didn't work.

Prepare it before the work starts, brief it to everyone who will do the work, and update it whenever conditions change. The [risk assessment template](/templates/risk-assessment) covers the assessment that sits alongside it.

## Who writes and signs off a method statement?

The contractor or subcontractor doing the work usually writes it, because they know the method best. Someone competent in the task, such as the supervisor or contracts manager, prepares it. A manager or health and safety adviser for that contractor reviews it, and the principal contractor reviews and accepts it before work starts. Everyone carrying out the work is briefed on it and signs the briefing record.

> **QS take:** From the commercial side, the method statement is often the first place a sequencing problem shows up. If the steps don't work in the order written, the programme and the price probably don't either. It's worth the QS or the planner giving it a read, not just the H&S lead.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a method statement a legal requirement in the UK?

Not by that name. The law requires you to assess the risks of the work and plan a safe system of work, under CDM 2015 and the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, and to record the significant findings of your risk assessment if you employ five or more people. A written method statement is the usual way to show how the work will be done safely, and most principal contractors make one a condition of starting.

### What is the difference between a RAMS and a method statement?

A method statement explains how a task will be done safely, step by step. A RAMS is a risk assessment and method statement in one document: the risk assessment identifies the hazards and rates the risk, and the method statement sets out the sequence and the controls. Most UK sites ask for the combined RAMS, which the [RAMS template](/templates/rams) covers.

### Who should sign off a method statement?

The person who prepares it signs it, a competent manager or health and safety adviser for the contractor reviews it, and the principal contractor reviews and accepts it before work starts. Everyone carrying out the work should be briefed on it and sign the briefing record. If the method changes on site, the revised version needs the same review before anyone works to it.

### How long should a method statement be?

As long as the task needs and no longer. A simple, low-risk task might fit on two pages, while a complex lift or a demolition can run much longer, with drawings and calculations attached. What matters is that every step, hazard and control is specific to this job and this site, and that the people doing the work can follow it.

### Can I use the same method statement on every job?

You can reuse the structure, but not the content without checking it. Every site has different access, neighbours, services, other trades and principal contractor rules, so each method statement needs reviewing and adapting before it's used. A generic sheet with only the task name changed is one of the most common reasons method statements get sent back.
